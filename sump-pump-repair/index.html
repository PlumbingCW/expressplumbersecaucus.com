Why pumps fail when you need them

Pumps fail for a short list of reasons.

Float stuck

Debris or a tangled switch keeps the pump off.

Power loss

Storms often cut power. A battery backup keeps it running.

Worn motor

Pumps wear out, often after years of use.

Clogged intake or discharge

Silt or a frozen discharge line blocks flow.

When a sump failure is an emergency

If water is within a few inches of the floor and the pump is silent, treat it as urgent.

  • Check whether the pump is plugged in and the breaker is on.
  • Lift the float to see if the pump starts. If it does, the float is the problem.
  • Do not stand in water near live electrical equipment.
  • Bail or wet-vac if you can safely.
  • Call the 24/7 line.

Installing or upgrading a pump

A pit with no pump, or one with an old pump, needs sizing for the water it must move. We check pit size, discharge routing and a check valve that keeps water from falling back. Pedestal pumps sit above the pit and are easier to service. Submersible pumps sit in the pit and are quieter. A battery backup or a water-powered backup gives protection when the power goes out.

Outdoor discharge and the winter risk

The discharge pipe should carry water away from the foundation and not pool near neighbors. In winter, a frozen or blocked discharge stops the pump. See the blog guide on outdoor sump discharge for routing questions, and call us if yours freezes.

Sump pumps and Secaucus flooding

On September 1, 2021, Ida flooded parts of Secaucus. The town's report listed Centre Avenue, Farm Road, Valley Court and Harmon Cove, and the town pumped out 91 basements. More than 100 homes had flooding. That is the practical case for a working sump pump and a backup in the lowest parts of town. The Town's own Ida recovery material included a basement flooding fact sheet, so ask the town for it if you were affected.

Not every wet basement is a sump problem. Some come from a sewer backup or groundwater through the wall. See basement waterproofing for the longer-term fixes.

Rules for the discharge pipe

Where sump water goes is not a free choice. Piping follows the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2021 edition, which New Jersey adopts as its plumbing subcode in N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, and many towns restrict discharge into the sanitary sewer, which is the town's concern through the Secaucus Municipal Utilities Authority. Ask the construction department before you change where the pump empties.
