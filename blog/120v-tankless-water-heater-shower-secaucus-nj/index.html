120V Tankless Water Heater for a Shower: Secaucus, NJ

A 120-volt tankless water heater cannot run a full shower in Secaucus, because the heating power needed is far beyond what a 120-volt household circuit can deliver. A shower at 1.5 gallons per minute with water heated about 55 degrees needs roughly 12 kilowatts. A 120-volt circuit supplies under 2 kilowatts. These small units are meant for a hand sink, not a shower.

This guide shows the arithmetic in plain terms, what a 120-volt unit is good for and what to install if you want a real tankless shower.

The arithmetic

Heating a gallon of water by one degree Fahrenheit takes about 8.3 BTUs. At one gallon per minute, raising the temperature 55 degrees takes about 27,000 BTUs per hour, or roughly 8 kilowatts. At 1.5 gallons per minute, it is about 12 kilowatts. A 15-amp 120-volt circuit delivers at most 1,800 watts, and a continuous load should stay below about 1,440 watts.

What it means in a shower

With 1.5 kilowatts available, a unit can heat only a trickle to shower temperature, around a quarter of a gallon per minute at that temperature rise. The result is a lukewarm, weak flow. Lower the flow and the temperature rise falls, but showering at a trickle is not what most people want.

What a 120V unit is good for

Small point-of-use units suit jobs where a little warm water is enough.

  • A hand sink in a bathroom or workshop far from the main heater.
  • A utility sink where a quick rinse is enough.
  • Pre-warming water at a single remote faucet.
  • Boosting a faucet that is far from the tank in an addition.

What a shower needs

Whole-fixture tankless heaters for showers are either electric units on high-amperage 240-volt circuits or gas units. An electric whole-house unit may need 100 amps or more of dedicated service, which often means an electrical panel upgrade. A gas unit needs gas line capacity and venting, covered in our guide to gas tankless installs. A small tank is often the sensible choice for a single remote bathroom.

Labels that mislead

Some product listings call a small 120-volt unit a shower heater. Read the specifications: the rated flow at your cold water temperature is the number to check. If the listing does not show gallons per minute at a stated temperature rise, be cautious.

Cold incoming water makes it worse

Winter water is colder, so the temperature rise needed is larger, and the same unit delivers even less. A heater that seems fine in summer may fail in January. Ask the manufacturer for the flow rating at the coldest water you will see.

A worked example

Say your cold water is 50 degrees and you want a 105 degree shower, a 55 degree rise. At 2 gallons per minute you move 120 gallons an hour. Each gallon takes about 8.3 BTU per degree, so 120 gallons times 55 degrees times 8.3 is about 54,800 BTU per hour, or roughly 16 kilowatts. Your outlet offers a tenth of that.

Gas tankless as the alternative

Gas units deliver far more heat from a smaller connection, which is why they are common for whole-home use. They need adequate gas supply, a vent to the outdoors and a condensate drain. In a condo the vent path may be the deciding factor, so ask the association before you plan one.

Low-flow fixtures help

A lower-flow showerhead cuts the heat needed. Dropping from 2.5 to 1.5 gallons per minute reduces the load by 40 percent. It does not make a 120-volt unit workable, but it helps any heater, and it lowers the water bill.

Questions to ask a seller

Ask for the rated flow at your winter water temperature, the circuit size required and whether the unit is approved for shower use. If a seller cannot answer, that is a sign to look elsewhere.

Tankless showers in Secaucus homes

Many Secaucus homes are condos and townhouses with limited electrical capacity, and Harmon Cove's 1970s buildings were not designed around a 240-volt tankless load. An electric whole-house unit may need a panel upgrade that the association has to approve. Permit rules vary, so ask the Secaucus construction department about electrical and plumbing work together. It is at the Town Hall Annex, 20 Centre Avenue, (201) 330-2027.

Cold-water temperature varies by season and by utility. Ask your water utility for its annual water quality report to see supply details.

The electrical limits are the same in North Bergen and Lyndhurst, because they come from physics, not from the town.

The electrical rule behind the numbers

The National Electrical Code limits a continuous load to 80 percent of a circuit breaker's rating, which is why a 15-amp, 120-volt circuit is treated as about 1,440 watts of continuous use, not 1,800. New circuits are electrical work, covered by New Jersey's electrical subcode, and they need a licensed electrician. Ask the Secaucus construction department about permits for both trades.
